# Cardboard Kingdom — a Costume Quest-led design direction

*Design direction from Alek (2026-10-12), kept here as the reference for future rounds. The original also came with an illustrated guide, an editable ~8,000-word guide and a reference-links ZIP, which are not in this repository. All new names and plot details in it are proposals, not canon.*

**Style direction (added 2026-10-13):** Costume Quest plus Animal Crossing for graphics, world settings, characters and general design: cozy villagers with personalities and name tags, lively water, trees to shake, cliffs with grass lips, cluttered homey towns, and Costume Quest's homemade-costume transformations and warm night lighting.

**Where the game stands against it** is tracked at the bottom: [Status in the game](#status-in-the-game).

---

I would make this a Costume Quest-style neighborhood adventure first, with rebuilding as the feature that makes it distinctly yours. The central pleasure should be exploring an inviting place with an odd little party, assembling homemade costumes, becoming magnificent in battle, and returning to see that your actions helped someone.

The guide develops 16 destinations across four seasons, 12 costume concepts, 16 engineering-puzzle families, the complete story arc, combat and co-op systems, and six rendering briefs. Its visual board includes 19 publisher-linked images and three official video sources, concentrated on Costume Quest. These are reference materials, not licensed production assets or new gameplay renders.

## 1. What to take from Costume Quest

Costume Quest's defining structure combines neighborhood exploration, party-building, collectible costumes, and transformations into powerful battle characters. That is the foundation to preserve, not merely its Halloween colors.

| Costume Quest-inspired priority | How Cardboard Kingdom should express it |
|---|---|
| Homemade becoming extraordinary | A shipping-box breastplate and ruler shield unfold into a magnificent cardboard guardian during battle. |
| Small places with personality | Each seasonal region contains a few connected neighborhoods, not an enormous open world. |
| A companion-driven adventure | Sam travels with two active friends whose observations and disagreements shape the journey. |
| Costumes as exciting discoveries | Completing an outfit unlocks a clear combat role and an exploration interaction. |
| Brief, funny conversations | Residents have small concerns, distinctive habits, and jokes unrelated to the main mystery. |
| Festivals and neighborhood activities | Reopening a parade route or repairing a theater gives rebuilding a celebratory payoff. |

The main loop should be:

> **Explore → meet someone → discover costume pieces → resolve an encounter → rebuild something useful → enjoy the changed neighborhood.**

Avoid separating these into unrelated modes. A quest to finish a kite costume should lead past a damaged crossing that the costume later helps inspect. A battle might protect a repair crew or stop a malfunctioning machine rather than simply interrupting travel.

The player should remember the people and places, not the resource requirements.

## 2. Give the story a stronger emotional structure

### Sam's problem should be understandable without a lecture

Sam still likes making things. What has become exhausting is the feeling that every assignment, correction, and unfinished model proves they are not good enough.

Open with a few concrete details: a crushed model bridge, an unanswered message from a classmate, and a notebook containing more corrections than drawings. A possible early line:

> "I used to make things because I wanted to see them work."

The recycling box appears beside discarded model-making supplies. Sam follows a small folded animal carrying a familiar scrap of their drawing, and lands inside an unfinished parade float. The townsfolk assume their replacement knight has arrived. That gives the opening vulnerability, mystery, and comedy without making it solemn.

### The Kingdom should matter beyond Sam

Establish that the Kingdom is a real place within the fiction, not a dream whose inhabitants disappear when Sam wakes up. The portal has transported discarded materials before. This explains why fragments of Sam's drawings appear in buildings and archives without making every resident merely a symbol of Sam's feelings.

Sam's engineering education provides useful vocabulary, but not all the answers. A ferryperson knows where floodwater actually travels. A carpenter understands a local material. A resident explains why a route must accommodate their cart.

Sam's development becomes:

> **"I should fix this myself" → "I should listen first" → "We can build this together."**

### Build a small, memorable companion group

| Companion | Personality | Their contribution to the story |
|---|---|---|
| Tansy, a dormouse costume maker | Inventive, talkative, delightfully untidy. | Helps Sam rediscover making things for pleasure rather than evaluation. |
| Brindle, a badger carpenter | Practical, dryly funny, occasionally too confident. | Demonstrates that experience matters, but familiar solutions are not always appropriate. |
| Mallow, a horned ferryperson | Quiet, kind, capable, displaced by flooding. | Challenges the "monster raider" story and contributes essential local knowledge. |
| Ibis, a magpie archivist | Meticulous, nervous, drawn to contradictory records. | Knows more about the secret society than they initially admit. |

Give each character interests unrelated to their thematic role. Mallow might collect terrible souvenir spoons. Brindle could be intensely competitive about flower arranging.

Use Sam plus two companions in battle, with painless switching at safe locations. Reserve companions should not fall behind in experience.

### Make the three twists support one another

**Twist one: the raiders are displaced neighbors.** Reveal this by early summer rather than saving it for the finale. Foreshadow it through behavior: the supposed raiders take blankets rather than valuables, protect a damaged toy, and retreat toward high ground. Their camp contains repaired cooking equipment and family pictures. The first misunderstanding should allow protective or de-escalating actions. Do not require players to repeatedly attack refugees and then criticize them for following the game's objectives. After the reveal, the task changes from protecting supplies against outsiders to designing a shared shelter and access route with the displaced residents.

**Twist two: the Society of the Perfect Fold is manipulating the floods.** The proposed society began as a useful standards guild. A controlling faction, led by Surveyor Quill, now insists that only its approved neighborhoods deserve protection. It blocks bypass channels, manipulates releases, conceals inconvenient records, and blames "improperly built" settlements. Its visual identity could use immaculate white card, identical folds, oversized approval stamps, and buildings that look impressive but ignore how people actually live. Do not make every inspector or engineer corrupt. Include a conscientious official who helps expose the wrongdoing. The conflict is control without care, not knowledge or standards themselves.

**Twist three: the endless storm embodies Sam's discouragement.** Keep this, but avoid the implication that Sam's burnout makes them morally responsible for everyone's suffering. A coherent explanation has three layers:

1. **Existing vulnerability:** settlements already have damaged or poorly maintained infrastructure.
2. **Deliberate wrongdoing:** the society makes the situation worse.
3. **The magical storm:** its weather device has absorbed the relentless corrections and self-criticism in Sam's discarded drawings, giving them form as the Last Cloud.

The cloud repeats things such as "Not ready," "Start again," and "Someone else would have done it better." It is an embodiment of discouragement, not evidence that struggling people harm others by feeling bad. The answer is not "be positive enough and the rain stops." It is to dismantle the harmful system, accept help, and make the valley capable of handling rain.

### Resolve the sunny-world/endless-storm contradiction

Keep the Last Cloud stalled over the upper watershed. Downstream towns still experience sunshine, beaches, festivals, and clear winter nights, while dangerous releases arrive from upstream. The seasons advance through chapters rather than the real-world calendar. Players should not wait months to see winter or worry that taking a break makes them miss a festival.

### End with resilience, not perfect weather

The finale should combine a short, theatrical battle against the weather machinery with a rebuilding sequence using familiar systems. The party stops the device, prepares a bypass, verifies downstream safety, and stages the release. Each region contributes something the player previously helped restore.

Then let it rain. The water follows the prepared routes. The shelter stays dry. The bridge remains open. People go about their lives.

When Sam returns home, the model is still imperfect and the degree is still a decision, not magically solved. They repair one corner, answer their friend, and take a manageable next step. Continuing the degree, reducing the workload, taking time away, or reconsidering the path can all fit the ending. Contentment should not mean accepting a situation that needs to change.

## 3. Four seasons and sixteen places to explore

Use four compact regional maps, each with four distinctive destinations, plus a recurring workshop hub called Patchwork Hall.

| Season and destination | Visual personality | Main activity or puzzle |
|---|---|---|
| **Spring** — Foldbrook Commons | Painted storefronts, bunting, puddles, budding trees. | Reopen the parade route through drainage and a small crossing repair. |
| Cherry-Tab Orchard | Tissue-paper blossoms and stacked fruit crates. | Redirect runoff without sending it into the neighboring schoolyard. |
| Puddlepost Schoolyard | Chalk drawings, paper pinwheels, oversized boots by the door. | Build a rain garden with a visible overflow route. |
| Willowfold Mill | Waterwheels, twine drives, patched timber-like cardboard. | Diagnose a blockage and restore a controlled water route. |
| **Summer** — Sunspool Quay | Bright awnings, spool bollards, improvised boats. | Restore loading access and organize a safe ferry approach. |
| Button Beach | Beach huts, bottle-cap furniture, colorful shade sails. | Move vulnerable facilities rather than assuming every shoreline needs a wall. |
| Reedwhistle Marsh | Green paper reeds, birds, sheltered water. | Rebuild a suitable living shoreline and a raised path. |
| Tin-Lid Ferryyard | Reused packaging, repair benches, communal cooking. | Help Mallow's community build an accessible connection to town. |
| **Autumn** — Amberwick High Street | Rust-colored leaves, warm windows, crowded shop displays. | Trace repeated flooding to a deliberately obstructed culvert. |
| Lantern-Lane Fair | Porch lanterns, costumes, folding stages, pumpkin stalls. | Recover costume pieces and repair the route for an evening procession. |
| Mothlight Archive | Tall shelves, hanging lamps, folded maps. | Compare records to discover where water has been diverted. |
| Switchback Gully | Steep paths, exposed supports, falling leaves. | Repair a crossing while investigating damage around its supports. |
| **Winter** — Snowcap Fold | White paper snow, blue shadows, bright scarves. | Assemble a suspension crossing with clear anchors and load paths. |
| Hearthparcel Refuge | Quilted walls, repaired roofs, communal tables. | Improve the shelter layout with its residents. |
| Whitewater Locks | Frosted mechanisms, warning lanterns, sheltered workstations. | Practice staged gate operation and downstream checks. |
| Crownspill Observatory | Survey models beneath the Last Cloud. | Disable the weather device and coordinate the final release. |

**Make day and night change the mood, not punish the player.** By day, reveal the handmade construction and busy community. At night, bring out porch lights, lantern trails, silhouettes, music, and quieter conversations. Allow players to advance to evening at a safe bench or gathering place. Do not make routine quests expire because someone spent too long exploring.

**Completed work should remain visible:** a repaired bridge carries deliveries, the rain garden gains butterflies in a later chapter, and a closed shop becomes a meeting place. Do not repeatedly destroy the player's improvements simply to generate more chores.

## 4. Costumes should remain the main progression system

Each costume needs three related expressions:

> **A homemade walking outfit → an exaggerated battle form → a recognizable exploration use.**

That transformation is where the Costume Quest influence should be strongest.

| Costume | Battle identity | Exploration or rebuilding interaction |
|---|---|---|
| Parcel Paladin | Protects an ally and redirects an attack. | Braces marked assemblies while another character positions a component. |
| Raincoat Ranger | Redirects a telegraphed splash and clears a wet status. | Deploys collection chutes at compatible locations. |
| Lantern Moth | Reveals hidden enemy intentions and supports allies. | Illuminates archives and follows light-marked routes. |
| Reed Warden | Creates a protective field and weakens rushing attacks. | Helps inspect wetland routes and place suitable planting modules. |
| Bridge Troll | Anchors the party and interrupts a heavy attack. | Moves or holds specified structural components. |
| Kite Courier | Repositions allies and performs a diving strike. | Carries a light guide line between designated points. |
| Pumpkin Pageant Marshal | Rallies the party with a theatrical flourish. | Leads festival activities and activates parade-related interactions. |
| Teapot Keeper | Restores an ally and improves the next assist. | Supports communal cooking and warmth-related story tasks. |

Optional concepts: Wind-up Crab, Snowplow Yak, Patchwork Dragon, Paper Astronomer.

For the Parcel Paladin, the exploration outfit might consist of a box breastplate, mailing-tube helmet, and ruler shield. In battle, those same elements unfold into a towering guardian with layered cardboard armor and glowing postage seals.

Collect three authored components through quests and exploration. Avoid random drops, endless scrap gathering, or inventories full of nearly identical materials. Once a useful field interaction is unlocked, keep it conveniently available through a shared kit or contextual swap. Players should not repeatedly open menus just to use the one costume that can light a lamp.

### Battle stamps: simple, expressive choices

Use original-style equipped stamps (Costume Quest 2's active Creepy Treat Cards are a different design reference, not an interchangeable name).

| Stamp | Proposed effect |
|---|---|
| Handle with Care | Your first Guard also protects a vulnerable objective. |
| Return to Sender | A successful defensive action can redirect a marked projectile. |
| Measure Twice | Inspect improves the next ally's action against that target. |
| No One Left Behind | Helping a fallen companion grants a temporary shield. |
| All Hands | Assisting another character strengthens their next special move. |

Begin with one slot per character, then introduce a second when players understand the system. Accessibility options must remain free settings, not stamps players need to equip.

## 5. Battles should feel theatrical, not exhausting

Use three active heroes, small enemy groups, visible enemy intentions, and a concise command set: **Act, Guard, Help, Inspect**, plus a costume-specific special.

A timing prompt should improve an action, not determine whether the character does anything at all. Start testing with full baseline effectiveness and a modest timing bonus. Include wider timing windows and automatic timing. Do not require rapid tapping or surprise sequences for mandatory actions.

| Encounter | Interesting decision |
|---|---|
| Runaway approval-stamp machine | Inspect its cycle, protect the repair crew, then interrupt the mechanism. |
| Animated scrap pile | Separate its supporting pieces rather than repeatedly attacking its health bar. |
| Frightened marsh creature | Guard and remove the source of distress before attempting an approach. |
| Society gate crew | Protect civilians while exposing the unsafe release sequence. |
| Last Cloud machinery | Coordinate costume roles while the rebuilding team prepares the bypass. |

Enemies can collapse into loose folds, lose their theatrical battle forms, retreat, or become calm. Avoid coding an entire species as enemies after establishing that "monsters" are people.

For Story Mode, allow independent settings for automatic timing, lower battle pressure, stronger hints, assisted placement, and recovery after defeat. No missed costumes, lost materials, or shameful "failure" grading.

**Co-op should give the guest real participation.** Prototype two-player shared-screen co-op, not four-player online play. The guest controls a real companion during exploration and battles. Control of the third party member can rotate or use a simple assist policy. During building, one player can propose a placement while the other inspects connections or prepares another component. Include undo, shared confirmation for consequential changes, and safe behavior when a player leaves. Never require simultaneous precision inputs to finish the main story.

## 6. Engineering puzzles: real relationships, not homework

The important learning is "Where does the water go?" and "What holds this up?", not memorizing equations. Use simplified, visibly explained systems rather than presenting the game as a real engineering design tool.

| Puzzle family | Player-facing task |
|---|---|
| Catchments and runoff | Trace water from roofs and paths to the place that floods. |
| Rain gardens | Place a collection area, then provide somewhere for excess water to go. |
| Swales and permeable surfaces | Change how water moves through a courtyard. |
| Storage and spillways | Hold some water while maintaining a controlled release route. |
| Floodgates | Prepare downstream conditions before opening a gate. |
| Culverts and blockages | Diagnose why a previously adequate route has stopped working. |
| Floodplain setbacks | Choose which activity should move rather than building a taller barrier. |
| Arch bridges | Arrange supports and arch pieces in a safe construction sequence. |
| Suspension bridges | Connect deck, hangers, cables, towers, and anchors. |
| Bracing and support siting | Stabilize a structure and keep vulnerable supports out of the wrong location. |
| Living shorelines | Select a suitable treatment for a sheltered shoreline. |
| Accessible routes | Make the finished connection usable by the residents who need it. |

(The full guide expands this into 16 families with maintenance diagnosis and combined-system puzzles.)

**"The picnic that keeps floating away."** A community's picnic area floods after rain. A resident suggests building a wall around it. Sam traces the flow and discovers that several roofs discharge toward the same path. The player redirects some runoff into a planted depression, improves the path, and creates an overflow route. The game should show temporary storage and movement, not a garden that makes unlimited water disappear (EPA's descriptions of rain gardens, bioretention, swales and permeable surfaces are a good foundation).

**"The very confident keystone."** Brindle insists that the keystone is the important part of an arch bridge. The player's inspection shows that the end supports are spreading. The solution is not simply adding a larger center block. It includes securing the abutments and following a supported assembly sequence. The joke is about overconfidence, not stupidity:

> Brindle: "The keystone was excellent."
> Tansy: "It had nowhere sensible to put its confidence."

**"A beach is not a marsh."** Give the player two nearby sites: an exposed beach and a sheltered reed-lined inlet. Do not make identical planting solve both (NOAA identifies sheltered shores as good living-shoreline candidates and emphasizes wave energy, tides and slope). This becomes a gentle lesson in choosing a suitable approach rather than unlocking one universally superior upgrade.

For all puzzles, let players **preview → test → observe → revise** without destroying the village. Essential explanations should be visible in the scene; deeper notes can live in Sam's optional notebook.

## 7. More things to do beyond fighting and rebuilding

The game needs moments when players enjoy the community rather than constantly rescuing it.

A **Neighbor Round** can adapt the pleasure of going door to door: return a borrowed tool, deliver a costume invitation, listen to a tiny story, or receive a stamp.

Seasonal activities could include paper-boat events in a controlled miniature channel, costume parades, seed swaps, lantern walks, friendly battle rehearsals, and helping prepare a performance in the restored theater.

Let players collect postcards showing locations before and after repairs, decorate Patchwork Hall with gifts, and assemble a season scrapbook. These activities should reward curiosity. They should not become daily obligations, relationship decay, or a requirement to complete every collectible before earning the good ending.

## 8. Graphics and rendering: stay close to Costume Quest

Recommended look: toon-shaded 3D characters with selective outlines inside softly lit, three-dimensional papercraft environments.

| Approach | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Toon characters + softly lit 3D world | Main direction. Strong party readability and room for atmospheric lighting. |
| Heavy cel shading everywhere | Test as an alternative; outlines may clutter structural details. |
| Photorealistic cardboard miniature | Reserve for selected promotional imagery, not the default gameplay target. |
| Flat paper puppets / 2.5D | Viable, but less faithful to the requested Costume Quest direction. |

Make the craft materials readable without making everything brown: corrugation on exposed cut edges, broad painted colors on large surfaces, subtle satin highlights on tape, and twine where a connection matters. Use fabric scraps and quilted pieces to soften homes and shelters. Give packaging fictional labels rather than recognizable commercial branding. The world can obey magical material rules while still conveying real structural relationships.

| Season | Day | Night |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Cream, blossom pink, fresh green, reflective puddles. | Soft violet shadows and warm porch lamps. |
| Summer | Turquoise water, coral awnings, bright sand, deep shade. | Amber dock lights and cool blue water. |
| Autumn | Rust leaves, ochre, plum accents, painted market stalls. | The strongest Costume Quest influence: glowing windows and lantern trails. |
| Winter | Pale blue shadows, white paper snow, colorful clothing. | Warm interiors, restrained snowfall, clear pools of navigational light. |

Use an elevated three-quarter exploration camera, with a gentle inspection view for construction. Do not hide puzzle relationships behind shallow depth of field or large foreground decorations. Sam should remain recognizably college-age. The animal cast can have exaggerated proportions, but should not look like traced Costume Quest characters.

Six rendering briefs (in the full guide): Foldbrook by day and night; the Parcel Paladin's outfit and battle transformation; Mallow's lived-in summer shelter; the autumn parade battle; a winter suspension crossing; and the repaired valley beneath gentle rain. Start by rendering the same Foldbrook layout from the same camera in two candidate styles.

## 10. What to build first, and one naming issue

Build **Foldbrook's Rainy Parade** as the first complete test: one neighborhood, three costumes, Sam and two companions, one timed-action battle, a runoff puzzle, a small crossing puzzle, and an evening celebration. Treat 25–35 minutes as a prototype target. The test succeeds when players can explain who needed help, how their solution worked, and why the celebration became possible.

**Naming:** *Cardboard Kingdom* should remain a working title. Chad Sell's existing graphic-novel series, *The Cardboard Kingdom*, already centers on homemade cardboard costumes. That warrants a separate naming review before branding and store-page production (this is not a trademark-clearance conclusion).

> The strongest version of this game does not end with Sam discovering a perfect world to escape into. It ends with Sam discovering that an imperfect world can still be worth caring about, and that building a better life does not have to be a solitary project.
